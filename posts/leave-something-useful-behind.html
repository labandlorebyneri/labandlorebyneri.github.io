Leave Something Useful Behind

On contribution, learning what you do not know, and leaving the work a little better than you found it.

By Neri September 15, 2026 Series: Loud & Load Lens: Creativity
Series
Loud & Load
Lens
Creativity
Published
Sep 15, 2026

Whenever I join a new organization, I find myself asking the same question:

How can I contribute and leave an imprint that lasts beyond my time there?

My answer has become surprisingly simple.

Do what I know.

Ask when I do not.

Learn.

Then do it all over again.

During my time at the Department of Information and Communications Technology, one of my responsibilities involved handling fund downloads.

I knew procurement. Fund downloading was another matter entirely.

I was clueless.

So I asked.

I learned from people who understood the work better than I did. I studied budget and internal policies, institutional rules and memoranda, and the laws that governed the process. I also sought the wisdom of the people who reviewed the documents, because understanding how something is evaluated can teach you how to prepare it better.

Then I kept doing it.

Ask. Learn. Apply. Improve. Repeat.

What began as something unfamiliar eventually became a process I could handle with greater confidence and understanding.

More importantly, the work was eventually registered as one of the organization's best practices.

That experience stayed with me.

It reminded me that contribution is not always about walking into a room already knowing what to do. Sometimes contribution begins with being willing to say, I don't know this yet. Who can teach me?

The same instinct has followed me into other engagements. It has shaped how I approach research and public conversation , how I contribute to education and development , and how I think about recognition through service .

The work changes. The habit does not.

Bring what you know.

Be humble enough to ask about what you do not.

Learn from the people, rules, systems, and experiences around you.

Then leave the work a little better than you found it.

Maybe that is one way a legacy begins.

Not by making sure an organization remembers your name.

Making sure something useful remains because you were.
