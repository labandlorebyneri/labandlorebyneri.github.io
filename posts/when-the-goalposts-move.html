When the Goalposts Move

On changing priorities, wasted effort, and why failure further down an organization can sometimes begin much higher up.

By Neri September 18, 2026 Series: Loud & Load Lens: Community
Series
Loud & Load
Lens
Community

Sep 18, 2026

I have been fortunate to work with people in positions of real authority.

Congressional representatives. CEOs. Founders. Directors. Senior leaders in government and private organizations.

Being close to people who make consequential decisions teaches you a lot about leadership.

It also teaches you how much one decision at the top can change the work of everyone below it.

Over time, one thing has become especially difficult for me to ignore.

Changing priorities do not frustrate me. Poorly communicated changing priorities do.

There is a difference.

I understand that organizations change direction. They have to.

New information comes in. A client changes their mind. A budget changes. A political environment shifts. Something urgent appears. A strategy that made sense three months ago suddenly does not make sense anymore.

I do not expect a leader to stick to a bad decision simply because it was the original plan.

In fact, I think being able to change your mind can be a sign of discernment, prudence, and integrity.

What I struggle with is when the people doing the work find out too late that the destination has changed.

You spend days building something.

Sometimes weeks.

You make decisions based on the information you were given. You coordinate with other people. You prepare documents. You build systems. You move things forward.

Then somewhere along the way, you discover that the priority changed.

Nobody really told you.

The goalpost simply moved.

And suddenly, the work that made sense yesterday is questioned today.

I have experienced this enough times to realize that organizations can easily label this as an execution problem.

Sometimes it is.

People misunderstand instructions. People miss deadlines. People make mistakes. People also have a responsibility to ask questions when something is unclear.

I believe in that responsibility.

But I also think leadership has to ask a harder question when people repeatedly fail to deliver what leadership expects.

Did they actually know what was expected?

That question sounds simple, but I think organizations skip it surprisingly often.

If the expectation changed, was the change communicated?

If the priority moved, did the people carrying the work know?

If the definition of success changed halfway through the project, was everybody working toward the same new definition?

Because if not, then the failure of the follower may also be a failure of leadership.

Not always.

But sometimes.

And I think leaders should be willing to examine that possibility.

Government made me particularly sensitive to this.

When I worked in public service, including work involving digital government coordination and broader digital transformation work , inefficiency never felt like an internal inconvenience to me.

The resources were public resources.

The money being spent came from taxpayers.

That changes the way you look at wasted effort.

A report that nobody needs anymore still took time to prepare.

A project that keeps being redesigned still consumed working hours.

A process started under one priority and abandoned under another still used public resources.

A meeting attended by ten people because nobody clearly communicated a decision also has a cost.

Those costs are easy to ignore because they do not always appear as a single line item saying, money wasted because people were not aligned.

But the cost is there.

Someone's time was spent.

A budget was used.

An opportunity was lost.

Something else could have been done instead.

That is what bothers me most about organizational waste. It is never only about the thing that was wasted. It is also about what that resource could have become.

In government, that could mean taxpayer money that might have supported another service, another program, another community, or another problem that actually needed solving.

Working around public institutions also taught me that decisions do not stay contained within a meeting room. They move through programs, documents, budgets, communities, and sometimes legislation itself.

That is one reason I became interested in the relationship between legislation, evidence, and institutional memory .

The private sector is different, but I eventually realized that the principle is not very different.

The money may belong to a founder, a company, or investors instead of the public.

But capital is still finite.

Every hour spent rebuilding something unnecessarily has a cost.

Every campaign repeatedly redirected without clear reasoning has a cost.

Every project that becomes urgent on Monday and irrelevant by Friday has a cost.

Every employee asked to redo work because an expectation was never properly communicated has a cost.

That money could have gone somewhere more useful.

A better system.

A better product.

A better solution.

A problem with greater impact.

This is why I sometimes react strongly when people describe constant redirection as simply being "agile."

I do not think agility means moving constantly.

Agility means being able to respond intelligently when circumstances change.

And intelligent response requires communication.

Otherwise, what looks like agility from the top can feel like chaos from everywhere else.

I have left organizations partly because of this.

Not because things changed.

I want to be clear about that.

I have worked in environments where change is unavoidable. Government changes. Technology changes. Clients change. Markets change. Priorities change.

I can work with change.

What becomes difficult is repeatedly investing time and effort toward one objective, then discovering that the real objective had already changed somewhere else.

There comes a point when that no longer feels like adaptation.

It feels like waste.

And waste is something I have a hard time accepting when I know what the same resources could have accomplished elsewhere.

Working with senior leaders has also changed the way I think about responsibility.

A leader does not need to know everything.

A leader is allowed to reconsider a decision.

A leader can say, We thought this was the right direction. We now know more, so we are changing it.

I respect that.

What I think leadership owes people is clarity about what happens next.

If the destination changes, tell the people travelling toward it.

If something is no longer important, say so.

If a project needs to stop, stop it before more resources disappear into it.

If expectations change, make the new expectations visible.

People cannot respond to information they do not have.

And that is where I think accountability sometimes becomes unfair inside organizations.

A decision is made at the top.

The consequences travel downward.

Then the people below are evaluated based on how well they handled a decision they may never have properly understood.

The employee gets blamed for producing the wrong output.

The project officer gets blamed for delay.

The team gets blamed for inefficiency.

The person doing the work is told they should have known.

Sometimes they should have.

But sometimes leadership should also ask whether they gave people a reasonable chance to know.

That distinction matters to me.

I do not believe followers should be passive.

People should clarify.

People should challenge assumptions.

People should communicate risks.

People should take ownership of what they are responsible for.

I have always tried to work that way myself.

But ownership has to exist on both sides of the hierarchy.

Some of what I have learned about this came from experiences that now sit within my leadership and development work , including moments where authority, responsibility, trust, and communication had to coexist in practice.

If one employee keeps failing, look at the employee.

If several capable employees keep struggling with the same expectation, perhaps look at the expectation too.

If an entire team seems confused about what matters, the problem may not simply be that everyone suddenly became bad at their jobs.

Sometimes the system is telling you something.

Sometimes people are telling you something.

And sometimes what looks like failure at the bottom is the accumulated effect of confusion at the top.

That may be one of the things leadership makes easy to forget.

The higher you sit, the less visible the cost of your uncertainty can become.

One unclear instruction may become several unnecessary meetings.

One late change in direction may erase weeks of work.

One assumption that "everyone already knows" may create ten different interpretations.

The leader moves on.

Everyone else carries the load.

Maybe that is why this belongs in Loud & Load.

Work is not only the tasks we are assigned.

We also carry unclear expectations.

We carry priorities that change halfway through the work.

We carry decisions we did not make.

We carry the pressure of trying to succeed against a definition of success that sometimes moves before we reach it.

Leadership cannot remove every uncertainty.

It cannot prevent every failure.

And it should not take responsibility for every mistake made by everyone underneath it.

But I think leadership does have a responsibility to create conditions where people have a fair chance to succeed.

Clear direction.

Clear priorities.

Clear changes.

Clear expectations.

Because when those things are missing, perhaps the question should not always be:

Why did the follower fail?

Sometimes the more useful question is:

What did leadership fail to make possible?
