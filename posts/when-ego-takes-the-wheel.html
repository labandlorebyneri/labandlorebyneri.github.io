When Ego Takes the Wheel

On flawed systems, human judgment, and what happens when leaders fear accountability more than failure.

By Neri September 21, 2026 Series: Loud & Load Lens: Character
Series
Loud & Load
Lens
Character
Published
Sep 21, 2026

There is always a flaw in a system.

Sometimes it is obvious. Sometimes it stays hidden until the right combination of pressure, timing, and poor judgment exposes it. The same is true of people. Human thinking is imperfect. We make assumptions, misread situations, become attached to our own ideas, and occasionally become too certain about things we do not fully understand.

Leadership does not make anyone immune to those flaws. If anything, it can make them more consequential.

A mistake made by one person may affect one task. A mistake made by someone with authority can redirect a team, consume a budget, weaken a process, or create a problem that everyone else is later expected to solve.

That is why I have become wary of what happens when ego takes the driver's seat.

The road does not suddenly become safer because the person behind the wheel has a bigger title.

I cannot deny that leadership has its perks.

There is power in being able to decide. There is influence in having people listen when you speak. There is convenience in having access to people, information, resources, and opportunities that others may need several layers of approval to reach.

I do not think pretending those things are unattractive makes anyone a better leader. They are part of the position.

But so are responsibility and accountability.

Those are the less glamorous parts.

Responsibility means recognizing that your decisions can affect people who had no part in making them. Accountability means being willing to examine your own role when those decisions do not produce the result you expected.

I have thought about this often through my own leadership and development experiences . Leadership becomes relatively easy to admire when things are going well. Its character becomes much clearer when something goes wrong.

A flawed system needs people who are willing to admit that the system has a flaw. A poor decision needs somebody willing to say that the decision may have been wrong. A leader who made the call has to be willing to ask whether the problem came from execution, communication, judgment, or perhaps from the decision itself.

Ego does not particularly enjoy those questions.

Ego usually offers something more comfortable.

Someone else misunderstood. The staff did not execute properly. The team was not ready. The circumstances changed. The people below should have known better.

Sometimes those explanations are true.

But when they become the explanation every time something fails, accountability begins to disappear from the top and accumulate at the bottom.

I wrote about the other side of this in When the Goalposts Move : sometimes what looks like a failure of the follower is also evidence of something leadership failed to communicate, clarify, or make possible. Ego makes that harder to see because it encourages us to look for fault everywhere except in our own decisions.

An organization becomes fragile when authority is protected from the consequences of its own decisions.

I learned some of this outside formal workplaces too. Experiences such as leading across generations forced me to think about authority less as a position and more as a relationship. People may recognize your role, but that does not mean they will automatically trust your judgment.

Trust has to survive disagreement.

The same lesson followed me into youth leadership . Having responsibility for other people does not suddenly make your thinking superior to theirs. Sometimes the people around you notice the weakness in a plan before you do.

The danger begins when a leader becomes unable to hear that.

Organizations often say they want people who think independently. They want initiative. They want honesty. They want people who are willing to speak up.

But speaking up only works when the organization can tolerate hearing something uncomfortable.

It is easy to welcome feedback when the feedback agrees with you. The real test comes when someone says that the strategy is not working, the target is unrealistic, the process has a weakness, or the leader's own decision may have contributed to the problem.

What happens after somebody says that tells people almost everything they need to know about the culture.

Do we examine the problem?

Or do we examine the person who pointed it out?

That difference matters because people learn very quickly what an organization rewards and what it punishes.

If admitting a mistake is dangerous, people learn to hide mistakes. If questioning a decision is treated as disrespect, people learn to keep quiet. If leaders defend every decision because reconsidering it feels like losing authority, the organization gradually loses its ability to correct itself.

This becomes particularly consequential in government.

Work involving digital government coordination and digital transformation reinforced for me that systems are rarely as simple as the person looking at them from one desk might imagine.

There are policies, resources, institutional constraints, competing priorities, people with different expertise, and citizens who eventually experience the consequences.

No single person sees all of it.

That is one reason systems have reviews, consultations, documentation, approvals, audits, and feedback mechanisms. They are not merely bureaucratic decorations.

At their best, they acknowledge a basic fact about human beings: none of us sees everything.

My work around legislation, evidence, and institutional memory has made that even harder for me to ignore. Decisions can outlive the meeting in which they were made. They enter documents, processes, budgets, programs, and sometimes institutional memory itself.

That makes the ability to question our own judgment more important, not less.

Experience helps.

Expertise helps.

Seniority helps.

None of them eliminates human fallibility.

In fact, power can sometimes make correction more difficult. People begin filtering what they say around you. Bad news becomes softer. Disagreement becomes more careful. Eventually, silence can start looking like agreement.

Authority can make a person feel more certain at exactly the moment they should be asking more questions.

That is where ego becomes expensive.

When ego takes the driver's seat, protecting the self can slowly become more important than protecting the organization.

The question changes from: What went wrong?

to: How do I make sure this does not look like my fault?

Those questions lead organizations in very different directions.

The first can lead to learning. The second usually leads to blame.

And blame is an expensive way to run anything.

People start spending energy protecting themselves instead of solving the problem. Meetings become exercises in positioning. Documents become shields. Decisions become harder to trace. Everyone knows something went wrong, but nobody wants to be the person standing closest to it.

An organization can survive that once.

Probably more than once.

But eventually the cost accumulates.

Trust weakens. Good people stop volunteering ideas. People who once cared about improving the work begin doing only what is required. The safest employee becomes the one who never takes a risk, and the safest opinion becomes whatever the most powerful person already believes.

Then one day leadership wonders why nobody speaks anymore.

Someone probably did.

Maybe nobody wanted to listen.

I have come to believe that one of the clearest signs of mature leadership is the ability to separate authority from infallibility.

You can be responsible for a decision and still admit that the decision was wrong.

You can hold a senior position and still ask someone junior, What am I missing?

You can change direction without pretending the old direction never existed.

You can receive criticism without treating criticism as disloyalty.

And you can hold other people accountable without using accountability as a way to protect yourself.

None of those things weakens leadership.

I think they make leadership more credible.

People do not need leaders who are never wrong. Those people do not exist.

They need leaders who make it possible for wrong decisions to be identified, corrected, and learned from before the consequences become larger.

There will always be flaws in a system.

There will always be blind spots.

There will always be decisions made with incomplete information.

And there will always be people, including leaders, who get something wrong.

The question is not whether failure can be eliminated.

It cannot.

The more useful question is whether an organization can recognize failure before ego turns it into something worse.

When responsibility is embraced, a mistake can become a lesson. When accountability is welcomed, a flawed system can become a better one.

But when accountability is feared, everyone else eventually pays the price.

The team. The organization. The business. Sometimes the public. And sometimes the people who trusted leadership with their time, money, effort, and confidence.

Leadership does come with power, influence, and convenience. I understand why those things are attractive.

But they are not the whole job.

If the car starts heading in the wrong direction, the first instinct cannot always be to blame the passengers.

Sometimes the leader has to look at the road.

Sometimes the leader has to look at the system.

And sometimes the leader has to look at their own hands on the wheel.
