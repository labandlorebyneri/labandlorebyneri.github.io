The Inconvenience of Thinking Twice

On inconvenience, discernment, and what we lose when reaction becomes faster than understanding.

By Neri September 24, 2026 Series: Lab & Lore Lens: Character / Creativity
Series
Lab & Lore
Lens
Character / Creativity
Published
Sep 24, 2026

I have started looking at inconvenience differently.

Not every inconvenience is something that needs to be removed immediately.

Sometimes it is something worth examining.

A process that takes longer than expected might be badly designed. But it might also contain a safeguard we do not yet understand. A person who keeps questioning an idea may be slowing the conversation down. They may also be seeing something everyone else has missed. A plan that refuses to work may not simply need more effort. It may be telling us that we misunderstood the problem.

Sometimes friction is information.

That does not mean every inconvenience deserves to stay. Some systems really are inefficient. Some rules are outdated. Some processes create unnecessary work. Some barriers should absolutely be removed.

But I have become increasingly interested in what happens before we decide which is which.

Do we understand the inconvenience?

Or do we simply want it gone?

That distinction matters because inconvenience forces us to pause, and pausing has become strangely difficult.

We live in an environment that rewards speed.

Something happens and someone records it. A screenshot appears. A short clip circulates. A fragment of a conversation reaches people who were never part of the original conversation.

Then the explanations begin.

Often before the evidence does.

People take sides. Someone becomes the villain. Someone else becomes the victim. Opinions harden. Screenshots are collected. Old posts are rediscovered. Context arrives later, if it arrives at all.

And there is an incentive to be early.

First to react.

First to condemn.

First to defend.

First to turn another person's conflict, mistake, or misfortune into a clever post.

When attention rewards certainty, what happens to discernment?

Discernment is inconvenient because it often asks us to do the opposite of what the environment rewards.

It asks us to wait.

To ask another question.

To distinguish what we actually know from what we have assumed.

To entertain the possibility that the first version of a story is incomplete.

Sometimes it even asks us to say something that sounds terribly unimpressive in an age of instant opinions:

I don't know enough yet.

There is a difference between seeing clearly and seeing quickly.

I have been reminded of that in work involving digital transformation across the archipelago . Consultation can be inconvenient when it complicates something that already looks complete on paper.

You can spend weeks developing a plan, present it to people, and discover that the assumptions underneath it do not survive contact with the people expected to live with the result.

That complication can feel like a setback.

But sometimes that is exactly what consultation is supposed to do.

The people closest to a problem may see something the people designing the solution cannot.

Their disagreement does not necessarily obstruct progress. Sometimes it improves the definition of progress itself.

The inconvenient question can therefore be more useful than the convenient agreement.

The same tension appears when we make judgments about people.

Public accountability matters.

There are times when public scrutiny exposes something that formal institutions ignored, minimized, or failed to address. There are reasons people speak publicly when they believe ordinary channels have failed them.

Silence can protect wrongdoing too.

But the failure of one system does not automatically make another system infallible.

A crowd can be wrong too.

This is what makes trial by publicity complicated for me.

Public attention can force necessary questions into the open. It can also create pressure to reach a conclusion before those questions have actually been answered.

Speed can expose injustice.

Speed can also manufacture it.

Accountability and punishment are not always the same thing.

Suspicion is not evidence.

Evidence is not automatically proof of every claim attached to it.

And emotional satisfaction is not necessarily justice.

This is one reason I keep returning to the role of evidence. In work around legislation, evidence, and local memory , I became more conscious of how consequential decisions become when they move beyond opinion and begin shaping institutions.

Evidence does not guarantee perfect judgment.

People interpret evidence. People select evidence. People disagree about what evidence means.

But evidence can force us to slow down long enough to ask an important set of questions.

What do we know?

How do we know it?

What are we assuming?

What would change our mind?

And what information is still missing?

Discernment is not the absence of judgment. It is the discipline that should come before it.

That distinction becomes even harder when groups are involved.

Groups are powerful.

They can create solidarity. They can mobilize help. They can make problems visible. They can create norms strong enough to change individual behavior.

But groups also create pressure.

If enough people believe something, disagreement can begin to feel socially dangerous.

If enough people repeat the same interpretation, repetition can start feeling like evidence.

And once a group has decided what a story means, information that complicates the story can become inconvenient.

My earlier work looking at community pantries through Filipino psychology reinforced for me that behavior becomes more understandable when we look at the relationships, values, expectations, and social conditions surrounding it.

People do not behave in isolation.

Neither do crowds.

That does not make collective judgment worthless. It simply means collective judgment deserves examination too.

We are especially vulnerable when the story gives us something we already want to believe.

This is where discernment becomes personal.

Once we publicly commit ourselves to one interpretation, reconsidering it becomes harder.

Changing our mind can begin to feel like losing face.

Admitting that we reacted too quickly can feel more uncomfortable than simply doubling down.

I explored a similar problem in When Ego Takes the Wheel , where the question was what happens when protecting our judgment becomes more important than examining whether that judgment was right.

Ego does not belong only to people with titles.

All of us can become attached to being right.

And the more publicly we announce that we are right, the more inconvenient reconsideration can become.

Sometimes thinking twice requires us to risk looking inconsistent so that we do not remain confidently wrong.

There is another reason I hesitate when a situation appears immediately obvious.

Sometimes the first story is not necessarily false.

It is simply incomplete.

A person may appear irresponsible without anyone seeing the instructions they were given.

A decision may appear irrational until the constraints behind it become visible.

Someone may appear to have failed when the conditions for success quietly changed around them.

That problem appears differently in When the Goalposts Move , where what looks like a failure of execution can sometimes begin with unclear direction, incomplete communication, or expectations that shifted without everyone knowing.

Context does not excuse everything.

Understanding why something happened does not mean approving of what happened.

But explanation and excuse are not the same thing.

We lose something important when we refuse to understand because understanding feels too close to forgiveness.

Discernment requires being able to hold several things in mind at once.

A person can do something wrong without every claim made about them becoming true.

A system can fail without every person inside it being malicious.

Someone can describe a genuine experience while still remembering parts of it imperfectly.

Two people can experience the same event differently.

New evidence can change what initially looked obvious.

Sometimes two uncomfortable things can be true at the same time.

None of this is satisfying if what we want is a fast verdict.

Complexity rarely performs well online.

"It depends" is not very shareable.

"I may need more information" does not attract the same attention as certainty.

Nuance is difficult to turn into a slogan.

But reality does not become simpler simply because simplicity performs better.

Discernment is the willingness to remain inside uncertainty long enough to make a better judgment.

That does not mean remaining uncertain forever.

Sometimes the evidence becomes clear.

Sometimes a conclusion is justified.

Sometimes something genuinely deserves condemnation.

Sometimes a person, organization, or system really does need to be held accountable.

Thinking twice does not require thinking endlessly.

It simply means the conclusion comes after inquiry rather than before it.

I think that matters beyond public controversies.

It matters in ordinary work.

The colleague who keeps asking questions may be annoying. They may also be preventing a mistake.

The additional review may feel unnecessary. It may also catch something the first review missed.

The evidence that complicates our preferred explanation may be exactly the evidence we most need to examine.

The discomfort we feel when someone disagrees with us is not automatically evidence that they are wrong.

And the easiest solution is not necessarily the solution that teaches us the most.

Maybe this is why I have begun treating some inconveniences as invitations.

Not invitations to suffer unnecessarily.

Invitations to notice.

To reconsider.

To ask whether the friction is revealing something about the system, the problem, the other person, or perhaps something about ourselves.

I do not want a world where we never judge anything.

Some things deserve judgment.

Some actions deserve consequences.

Some systems need to be challenged, changed, or replaced.

But I also do not want speed to become a substitute for wisdom.

There is a difference between seeing clearly and seeing quickly.

Maybe thinking twice feels inconvenient because it asks something that our current environment rarely rewards: patience without passivity, uncertainty without indifference, and judgment without the rush to perform it.

Not every inconvenience deserves to remain.

But before we remove it, condemn it, fix it, or turn it into a verdict, perhaps it is worth asking one more question.

What is this inconvenience trying to make me notice?
