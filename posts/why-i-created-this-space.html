Why I Created This Space

A note on why I resist treating creativity and scientific thinking as opposites, and why I wanted somewhere to examine what happens when they meet.

By Neri September 10, 2026 Series: Lab & Lore Lens: Creativity
Series
Lab & Lore
Lens
Creativity
Published
Sep 10, 2026

It is often assumed that creativity and science live on opposite ends of a spectrum. One is imagined as intuitive, expressive, and unpredictable. The other is treated as logical, measured, and disciplined.

I have never found that distinction particularly convincing.

Some of the most enduring creative work has depended on structure, proportion, geometry, observation, technique, and measurement. At the same time, good science requires more than following rules. It requires someone to notice a problem, imagine an alternative explanation, ask a question that has not been asked in quite the same way, and design a way to investigate it.

Even artificial intelligence makes this tension interesting. What can appear creative on the surface emerges from computation, rules, data, probability, and engineered systems.

Creativity does not have to compete with rigor. It can be one of the things that makes rigor useful.

The questions I keep returning to

I am interested in how people think, decide, create, organize, communicate, and interact with technology.

Sometimes the unit of interest is one person. Sometimes it is a group, an organization, a society, or a movement.

The scale changes, but many of the questions remain connected. What shapes a person's values? Why do groups normalize certain behaviors? Why do organizations repeat solutions that no longer work? Why do some ideas spread while others disappear? How does technology change not only what people can do, but what they expect from themselves and from one another?

Those questions eventually gave me three broad lenses through which to organize the things I want to explore.

Character. Creativity. Community.

Character is the individual. It is the psyche, identity, values, interests, attitudes, morality, pain, suffering, decisions, and experiences that shape who a person becomes.

Creativity is the response. It is how people generate alternatives, solve problems, communicate, experiment, adapt, market ideas, and respond when the familiar answer is no longer enough.

Community is the collective. It is what happens when individual behavior enters groups, organizations, institutions, hierarchies, economies, societies, and movements.

I do not think these three exist independently. Character influences creativity. Creativity can reshape community. Community, in turn, influences the character of the people who live inside it.

Why a lab?

A lab is not interesting because it already has the answer. It is interesting because it gives questions somewhere to be examined.

For me, the lab represents observation, questioning, experimentation, research, comparison, revision, and the willingness to discover that an assumption was wrong.

Not everything I write here will be a finished argument. Some ideas will be observations. Some will be working theories. Some may change after I learn more.

That is intentional.

And why lore?

Lore is what survives experience.

It can be knowledge, story, interpretation, warning, memory, contradiction, or something a generation decides is worth carrying into the next.

The problem with lore is that it can become detached from the questions that produced it. We inherit conclusions without always inheriting the investigation.

I want this space to preserve both.

The lab investigates. The lore remembers.

Lab & Lore is therefore less a collection of answers than a record of inquiry.

I want to use it to test ideas, document observations, think through problems, challenge my own assumptions, and preserve what seems worth remembering.

What is understood in the lab becomes lore for another generation to encounter, question, challenge, and build upon.
