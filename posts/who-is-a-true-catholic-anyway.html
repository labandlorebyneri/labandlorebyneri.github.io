Who Is a True Catholic Anyway?

On faith, performance, exploitation, dignity, and the distance between what we profess and how we live.

By Neri September 27, 2026 Series: Love & Lord Lens: Character
Series
Love & Lord
Lens
Character
Published
Sep 27, 2026

Faith can be beautiful.

It can give people comfort, belonging, meaning, and something to hold onto when life becomes difficult to explain.

But faith can also be useful in a more troubling way.

It can become a cover.

Religious language can make an intention appear noble. A Bible verse can make a position sound morally certain. The language of service can make control look like sacrifice. Obedience can be invoked when what is really being demanded is silence.

Even exploitation can be dressed in the language of virtue.

Work becomes "service."

Enduring mistreatment becomes "sacrifice."

Staying quiet becomes "humility."

And questioning any of it can be made to look like a failure of faith.

Religion becomes dangerous when its moral language is used to make exploitation look virtuous.

That is where I struggle.

Because who is a true Catholic anyway?

Is it the person who knows Scripture and quotes it often?

The person who never misses Mass?

The person who speaks uncomfortable truths?

The person who publicly defends Catholic values?

Or is it the person who simply says, I believe in God, and then spends a lifetime imperfectly trying to live up to the promises, mysteries, demands, and contradictions of that faith?

I do not know.

Maybe none of us can know completely.

We can see actions.

We can hear words.

We can notice patterns.

We can recognize contradictions between what someone says and what someone does.

But another person's conscience, intention, and relationship with God are not ours to inspect completely.

I have written about faith more personally in Another Reason I Created This Space , where love, memory, loss, and belief became inseparable from the way I understood what this space was for.

Maybe that is why I have become less interested in trying to determine who looks religious enough.

I am more interested in what faith seems to produce in a person's life.

The loudest profession of faith is not always the one spoken aloud. Sometimes it is the life built around it.

For me, faith becomes most convincing in the way a person moves through the world.

In the sense of purpose they carry.

In the way they serve.

In the way they recognize another person's dignity regardless of title, income, influence, usefulness, or social standing.

And perhaps most especially, in how they treat people who have very little power to reward them for doing the right thing.

The poor.

The powerless.

The oppressed.

But also the people we encounter every day.

The workmate who made a mistake.

The staff member who cannot answer back.

The stranger who has nothing to offer us.

The person whose name will never open a door for us.

Some of the experiences I now place under community and service taught me that service becomes much more meaningful when it stops being about what the giver receives from it.

That became tangible in experiences such as Paskong Katutubo and the legal and medical mission .

Service brings you into contact with people whose circumstances may be very different from your own.

Their dignity is not.

The loudest profession of faith may be the dignity we give to people who have nothing to offer us in return.

But faith is not tested only in formal acts of service.

I think it is tested in places that do not look religious at all.

In a conversation.

In the way we speak to someone when we are irritated.

In whether we keep a promise after keeping it becomes inconvenient.

In whether we admit fault when doing so may cost us pride, status, comfort, or advantage.

In whether we remain respectful when we have more power in the room.

In whether we choose honesty when dishonesty would be easier.

These moments are ordinary.

They rarely look like public declarations of faith.

There is no ceremony in them.

Usually no audience either.

Maybe that is precisely why they matter.

It is easy to perform faith when faith is visible. It is much harder to live it when nobody is watching, when there is nothing to gain, and when doing the right thing asks something from us.

This is also where faith and accountability begin to meet.

In When Ego Takes the Wheel , I wrote about what happens when protecting our judgment becomes more important than examining whether that judgment was right.

I think faith makes that question even more uncomfortable.

If humility is something we claim to value, what happens when humility requires us to say: I was wrong?

If truth matters, what happens when the truth is inconvenient?

If respect for human dignity matters, what happens when the person in front of us has less power than we do?

If service matters, what happens when nobody is there to recognize the service?

These are much harder questions than whether someone can quote Scripture.

Virtue signalling becomes tempting precisely because the appearance of virtue is easier to manage than the discipline of living it.

We can speak about compassion and still be cruel.

We can speak about honesty while telling only the version of events that protects us.

We can praise humility while needing recognition for every good thing we do.

We can speak about service while treating the people serving us as though their dignity depends on their position.

We can even use faith to place ourselves above other people morally.

And that is where I have to be careful too.

Because it would be easy to write this entirely about other people.

The people who quote Bible verses but behave differently when no one is watching.

The leaders who talk about service but fear accountability.

The people who use morality as a language for judging everyone except themselves.

But Christianity becomes fairly useless to me if I use it only as a framework for diagnosing everyone else's hypocrisy.

The harder question is whether I do it too.

That question brings me back to service.

I have also encountered this tension in service learning , where service becomes more meaningful when it is understood less as an event and more as something that asks the person serving to examine themselves too.

Service is not automatically virtuous simply because we call it service.

Motivation matters.

Power matters.

The dignity of the person being served matters.

And so does the difference between helping someone and using someone's circumstances to make ourselves appear helpful.

This is also why I think differently now about recognition through service .

Recognition can be meaningful.

It can affirm work.

It can remind us that contribution matters.

But recognition cannot be the reason service becomes meaningful.

The value of service cannot depend entirely on whether someone was watching.

This is where I return to the question in the title.

Who is a true Catholic anyway?

I still do not know.

And I do not think that judgment belongs to me.

Faith is too interior, too complicated, and too personal for me to believe I can inspect another person's soul from the outside.

But I do think our actions reveal something about what our professed values have actually formed in us.

If faith teaches dignity, then it should become visible in the dignity we give others.

If it teaches service, it should become visible in what we are willing to do for people who cannot repay us.

If it teaches humility, it should become visible in our willingness to admit when we are wrong.

If it teaches truth, it should become visible when truth becomes inconvenient.

And if it teaches love of neighbor, then perhaps the real test is not how loudly we say we believe it.

Perhaps the test is how another person experiences us when they are standing in front of us.

The more I think about it, the less interested I become in who looks religious.

I am more interested in what faith does to a person.

Does it make them gentler without making them weak?

More truthful without becoming cruel?

More accountable without becoming self-righteous?

More willing to serve without needing to be seen serving?

More respectful of people whose status offers them nothing in return?

That, to me, feels closer to the point.

The loudest profession of faith may not be a verse.

Not a prayer.

Not a title.

Not a public declaration.

It may simply be the way we treat people when there is nothing to gain from treating them well.
