Resistance

Collective intentions meet real limits.

Community work often begins with care, urgency, or a shared sense that something should be done. But intention alone does not determine what happens next. Access, trust, resources, timing, authority, and participation all shape what a group can actually sustain.

Service makes those forces easier to see. It reveals who gets heard, who gets reached, who carries the work, what people are willing to contribute, and where even a good intention meets friction.

Through Community, this becomes Resistance: not opposition for its own sake, but the pressure that exposes what collective life is really made of.

From service to resistance

Friction is information.

Resistance in community work is not always conflict. It can appear as low participation, mistrust, limited access, competing priorities, uneven responsibility, or systems that make a simple action harder than it should be.

Those points of friction are useful because they show where the real work is. They reveal what a group values, what it can coordinate, what it avoids, and what conditions need to change before collective intention can become collective action.
