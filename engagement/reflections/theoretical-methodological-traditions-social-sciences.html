Theoretical and Methodological Traditions of Social Sciences

A Soc Sci 1 essay examining why the social sciences are sciences in their own right, how their theoretical and methodological traditions developed, and why their contribution to knowledge is as consequential as that of the natural sciences.

Central argument

Scientific rigor does not require every science to study reality in the same way.

The essay begins from the premise that the social sciences, like the natural sciences, are engaged in knowledge production. Their distinctive contribution lies in examining human beings, institutions, meanings, cultures, and societies while also asking how knowledge itself is produced.

Rather than treating methodological plurality as evidence that the social sciences are less scientific, the paper presents that plurality as a response to the complexity of human experience and to debates about whether reality is discovered, interpreted, or co-constructed.

Five major paradigms

The essay maps a continuum of ways to understand knowledge.

These traditions differ in how they understand truth, objectivity, context, and the relationship between researcher and reality.

01

Positivism

Reality is treated as existing independently of the observer, with knowledge discovered through rigorous, clearly defined procedures.

02

Post-positivism

The possibility of a reality beyond the observer is retained, while recognizing that knowledge is shaped by the contexts of researchers and participants.

03

Critical realism & contextualism

Knowledge is understood as historically, culturally, and situationally conditioned rather than universally detached from context.

04

Social constructionism

Meaning and reality are treated as inseparable from language, culture, history, and the ways people collectively make sense of experience.

Theoretical roots

The social sciences emerged in conversation with the natural sciences, not outside science.

The paper traces the modern social sciences to a period when scientific legitimacy was strongly associated with the procedures and assumptions of the natural sciences. Positivism therefore became one important route through which the social sciences established themselves as systematic fields of inquiry.

It also critiques a rigid natural-versus-social binary. Drawing on the essay's discussion of essentialism, the paper argues that the blurred boundaries between these domains helped produce a broader spectrum of scientific paradigms rather than two completely separate forms of knowledge.

Changing science

Methods evolved as societies, politics, and research problems changed.

The essay treats theory and method as historically responsive rather than fixed.

01

Feminism

Feminist approaches challenged knowledge traditions dominated by male perspectives and expanded whose experiences could shape research questions and interpretation.

02

Phenomenology

Phenomenology foregrounded first-person experience and the meanings people attach to the phenomena they live through.

03

Indigenization

Indigenization questioned the assumption that knowledge produced in Western settings automatically explains non-Western realities, using Sikolohiyang Pilipino as a key example.

04

Intersectionality

Intersectionality drew attention to how research and lived reality are shaped by overlapping social identities, including identities often underrepresented in dominant knowledge traditions.

Cognitive justice

The paper ends by arguing for a more plural and democratic science.

The essay presents cognitive justice as a response to the dominance of Western knowledge traditions and to problems of underrepresentation, misrepresentation, and the uneven distribution of authority in global research.

Its conclusion is not that scientific standards should be abandoned. Instead, it argues that science becomes stronger when different knowledge traditions can enter into dialogue and when non-Western cultures, languages, and intellectual traditions are recognized as legitimate sources of scientific understanding.

Why the social sciences matter

Their relevance lies in making human realities intelligible without reducing them to one method of explanation.

Across the essay, the social sciences are presented as rigorous precisely because they develop methods appropriate to the questions they ask. Human behavior, culture, power, identity, and meaning are observable and researchable, but they also require attention to context and interpretation.

The paper therefore positions the social sciences alongside the natural sciences as essential knowledge-producing fields. Their objects of study differ, but their contribution to understanding and improving human life is no less substantial.
