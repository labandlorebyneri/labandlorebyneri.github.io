The Ecological Nature of Prejudice and Discrimination

A Psych 180 reflection for the Social Psychology module on Prejudice and Discrimination, examining how bias moves between individual attitudes, social institutions, laws, media, education, family, and collective life.

Psych 180 · Social Psychology

Central question

Why do prejudice and discrimination persist even after repeated efforts to reduce them?

The reflection begins by distinguishing prejudice as a negative attitude or prejudgment from discrimination as unjust behavior shaped by prejudice and stereotypes.

It then asks whether prejudice and discrimination can be reduced by looking beyond individual beliefs and examining the environments in which those beliefs are learned, reinforced, and acted upon.

An ecological view

The paper treats prejudice as something produced across people, institutions, and systems.

The essay argues that prejudice and discrimination cannot be understood only as personal failings. It describes government, education, media, religion, and family as major social institutions that help construct norms, identities, expectations, and ideas about which groups are accepted or marginalized.

Examples in the original paper are used to show how laws, schooling, media representation, religious belief, and family socialization can reinforce stereotypes or discriminatory practices. These examples reflect the paper's 2022 academic context and are presented as part of its original argument.

How the argument develops

Four levels of response organize the reflection.

The paper moves from systemic reform to interpersonal contact and personal self-examination.

01

Institutional reform

The essay argues that laws and policies can challenge institutional discrimination and help change the norms that shape public behavior.

02

Empathy and perspective-taking

Empathy building and role playing are presented as ways of reducing prejudice by encouraging people to understand the experiences of groups they may stereotype or exclude.

03

Intergroup contact

The reflection discusses Allport's contact hypothesis and the idea that meaningful interaction between groups can reduce anxiety, correct assumptions, and create opportunities for mutual understanding.

04

Self-awareness and discourse

The paper ends at the individual level by emphasizing reflection, challenging one's own biases, and responding to discriminatory behavior through dialogue rather than disengagement alone.

Collective action

The reflection connects social change with law, policy, and shifts in social norms.

The paper cites Philippine laws protecting women and people living with HIV as examples of how legal mechanisms can respond to institutional forms of discrimination.

Its broader point is that individual attitude change and institutional reform operate together. Changes in law can influence norms, while changes in everyday beliefs and relationships can shape what institutions are expected to protect.

Personal reflection

Awareness is treated as the starting point rather than the endpoint.

The essay acknowledges that people can reproduce prejudice without always recognizing it. Its response is not to treat awareness as proof of moral completion, but as a reason for continued reflection and correction.

The closing logic of the paper is relational: discourse can create understanding, understanding can support empathy, and empathy can help weaken prejudice.

Conclusion

The reflection argues for change at both the individual and collective level.

The paper concludes that prejudice and discrimination remain deeply embedded in social life, but are not beyond intervention.

Its final position is that legal reform, institutional change, empathy, intergroup contact, self-awareness, and sustained collective effort can work together toward greater diversity, inclusion, and equality.
