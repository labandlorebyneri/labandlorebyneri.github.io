Ang Maglingkod at Hindi ang Paglingkuran

A reflection on service-learning at He Cares Mission, where empathy, pakikiramdam, kapwa, faith, and the practice of service became less abstract because they had to be lived with other people.

← Back to Education & Development

Context

Psychology 108 moved learning outside the classroom.

The original reflection came from a Lingkod Aral experience with He Cares Mission, an organization caring for children and supporting children in the surrounding community.

I joined as a volunteer teacher. Sessions included energizers, lessons, activities, meals, conversation, and the slower work of becoming familiar enough with one another for the children to participate more freely.

The placement was cut short when the pandemic and community quarantine began, but the experience had already become a way of testing ideas I had encountered in psychology against an actual relationship.

What I was seeing then

Difference was the first thing I noticed. Relationship changed what I did with it.

In the original essay, I repeatedly described the children and myself as coming from “different worlds.” I was trying to name an obvious socioeconomic distance: the resources, expectations, and forms of security available to us were not the same.

But repeated contact complicated that first framing. I also noticed ordinary acts of care: children saving food for family members, encouraging classmates to participate, guiding one another back into activities, and finding ways to remain connected.

The experience began to move from comparison toward relationship. Difference did not disappear, but it stopped being the only thing I could see.

Empathy in practice

Knowing the word was easier than knowing how to behave because of it.

The strongest thread in the paper is empathy. At the time, I wrote that the placement tested whether I understood empathy not only as a concept but in practice.

That meant becoming more careful about how I approached the children, how I interpreted what I saw, and how I adjusted my own behavior so that the socioeconomic distance I was conscious of did not become the center of every interaction.

Flexibility, pakikiramdam, and patience became practical skills rather than traits I could simply claim to possess.

Theory in lived experience

Psychological concepts became useful because they helped name a changing relationship.

The original reflection used Filipino Psychology to interpret what I was observing, particularly kapwa and the changing level of relationship between volunteer and child.

The reflection connected everyday concern for family and others to the idea of kapwa as a core Filipino value shaping social relations.

Pakikiramdam

Working with children required reading tone, hesitation, comfort, participation, and the social atmosphere instead of relying only on the lesson plan.

What I understood then

Service was changing the person doing the serving, too.

I organized the original reflection around changes I experienced psychologically, socially, mentally, emotionally, and spiritually.

The placement strengthened my sense of empathy, tested my ability to adapt to people and situations, clarified why psychology mattered to me, and reconnected service with a faith tradition that had shaped much of my earlier life.

At that stage, I understood service partly as formation: an encounter that sharpened character and made purpose more concrete.

What I see differently now

Empathy is not the same as making another person's hardship part of my own growth story.

Reading the essay again, I can see how easily reflection can center the volunteer. I wrote about what the children taught me, how the experience changed me, and how their circumstances clarified my own purpose.

Those changes were real, but they are not the measure of whether the service was good. The children were not there to become examples, lessons, or proof of my empathy. They had their own relationships, agency, needs, humor, faith, and ways of understanding their lives.

I would also be more careful today with language that divides people into “different worlds.” Material inequality matters and should not be softened, but emphasizing distance can accidentally make difference feel more essential than it is.

The more useful lesson now is that empathy requires both closeness and restraint: enough attention to understand another person's reality, and enough self-awareness not to make oneself the center of it.

Reflection

Service becomes formative when reflection does not stop at good intentions.

The original paper captured how I understood the experience at the time. Returning to it now makes another kind of learning possible: preserving what was sincere while also examining the assumptions, language, and position from which I was writing.
