Why do suffering, inequality, and conflict persist?

A Soc Sci 1 essay examining how systems of power, especially colonialism, imperialism, and capitalism, shape suffering, inequality, conflict, and competing ideas of development.

← Back to Essays

Essay record

The essay at a glance.

Academic context
Soc Sci 1 · Foundations of Social Science · University of the Philippines
Essay type
Critical social science essay
Theme
Power, development, inequality, and self-determination
Central argument
Suffering, inequality, and conflict persist when systems and relationships concentrate power and define progress unevenly.
Structure
Power and greatness → colonialism → imperialism → capitalism → development → self-determination

Central argument

The essay locates suffering, inequality, and conflict within systems built around power and control.

The paper begins from the argument that individuals and societies often pursue power and greatness, and that this pursuit has historically been organized through systems such as colonialism, imperialism, and capitalism.

Within the essay's framework, these systems do not merely produce isolated hardships. They shape relationships between countries, classes, cultures, and social groups in ways that reproduce unequal access to resources, influence, and opportunity.

How the argument develops

Three systems organize the paper's explanation.

The sections below summarize the original Soc Sci 1 essay's interpretation of how power operates across historical and economic systems.

01

Colonialism

The essay describes colonialism as direct control over a territory and its people for the benefit of an external power. The Spanish colonial period in the Philippines is used to discuss exploitation, hierarchy, cultural domination, and the production of inferiorized identities.

02

Imperialism

The paper presents imperialism as a system in which political, economic, and cultural influence can continue even without the same form of direct colonial rule. American influence in the Philippines is discussed through governance, trade, cultural aspiration, and economic dependence.

03

Capitalism

The essay argues that capitalist systems can reproduce inequality through private control of resources and labor, unequal wages, competition, and exclusion shaped by class and other social identities.

The Philippines as historical example

The paper connects political domination with cultural and psychological consequences.

The Spanish colonial period is used to illustrate how control over land, labor, religion, and social classification can produce material hardship alongside internalized ideas of inferiority.

The essay then turns to the American period to discuss cultural hegemony and economic influence, arguing that political independence does not necessarily eliminate unequal relationships between a formerly colonized society and a more powerful state.

Development and power

The essay questions who gets to define what development should mean.

Drawing on Wolfgang Sachs, the paper links postwar ideas of development with the expansion of American influence and with the assumption that less powerful countries should follow a model established by wealthier states.

In the essay's analysis, this creates a problem when development becomes measured primarily through standards defined by dominant countries rather than through the histories, cultures, priorities, and needs of the societies being evaluated.

Self-determination

The paper ends by returning the definition of development to the people who live within it.

The essay's proposed direction is self-determination. Drawing on Philpott, it argues that people should have meaningful authority over the political, cultural, economic, and educational systems that shape their lives.

Rather than allowing an external power to decide what counts as progress, the paper argues that development should emerge from the people themselves and from their capacity to define what strengthens their society.

Conclusion

Suffering, inequality, and conflict are presented as interconnected rather than separate problems.

The essay concludes that suffering, inequality, and conflict persist because they are reproduced through systems and relationships that concentrate power and define progress unevenly.

Its final position is that changing those conditions requires societies to reconsider inherited definitions of development, recover the ability to define their own priorities, and strengthen self-determination.
