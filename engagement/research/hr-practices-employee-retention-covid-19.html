Research Manuscript · BA 152

Human Resource Practices and Employee Retention during the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Correlation Study

A descriptive quantitative study examining how recruitment, training and development, performance appraisal, and compensation and benefits were associated with employee retention during the COVID-19 pandemic, with the analysis focused on manufacturing and retail organizations.

The questions

Which human resource practices were associated with employee retention during the pandemic?

The study examined four areas of human resource management that were disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic: recruitment, training and development, performance appraisal, and compensation and benefits.

It asked which recruitment strategies were being used, which training and development programs were associated with retention, and which performance-management, compensation, and benefit practices were associated with retention in manufacturing and retail organizations.

Method

The study used organization-level survey data and correlation-based analysis.

The researchers used a structured questionnaire developed by the course instructor and distributed it to human resource managers and other organizational representatives between November and December 2021.

The paper focused its sector analysis on 18 respondents from manufacturing and retail organizations. Eight respondents represented manufacturing organizations and ten represented retail organizations.

Employee retention was operationalized using the ratio of respondents' estimated average number of employees in 2021 to their estimated number of employees in 2020. Specific HR practices were coded as categorical variables.

The analysis used chi-square tests and Cramer's V for categorical associations, point-biserial correlations for categorical-continuous relationships, and regression analyses to test whether initially observed associations remained statistically significant.

What the study found

Most broad HR-practice categories were not significantly associated with retention, but several specific practices stood out.

The findings below follow the manuscript's reported statistical analyses. Where the manuscript uses inconsistent wording about the direction of an effect, this summary follows the reported coefficients and regression results rather than reconciling them silently.

01

Recruitment practices were largely independent of industry type

Most recruitment practices did not show a significant relationship with industry type. University job fairs produced an initial significant chi-square result, but Cramer's V indicated little to no association.

02

Skills enhancement training showed a bivariate relationship

Within the manufacturing and retail subset, skills enhancement training was positively correlated with retention in the initial analysis, but the relationship did not remain statistically significant in the multiple-regression model.

03

Performance-management practices were not significant

The study did not establish a statistically significant relationship between the performance-management practices examined and employee retention, either across industries or within manufacturing and retail.

04

Reduced working hours remained significant in regression

Reduction of working hours was the only variable that remained statistically significant in the final regression analysis of retention rate. The reported coefficient was negative, indicating an inverse relationship in the model.

Additional patterns

The manuscript also identified sector-specific signals that did not all survive regression testing.

Within manufacturing and retail, freezing pay raises showed a significant negative bivariate association with retention. The relationship was not significant once included in the multiple-regression analysis.

The paper also reported that manufacturing and retail organizations generally rated their HR-development performance positively during the pandemic, while many organizations were still in the process of recovery.

The descriptive results further showed differences in contingency planning and pandemic-response practices between manufacturing and retail respondents, although these descriptive patterns were not themselves evidence of causal effects on retention.

Interpretation

The study suggests that retention during crisis conditions may depend on a wider set of organizational and employee factors than individual HR practices alone.

The manuscript concludes that the broad categories of training, performance appraisal, and compensation and benefits did not show a general significant relationship with employee retention in the manufacturing and retail sample.

At the same time, individual practices such as skills enhancement training, pay freezes, and reduced working hours produced signals in parts of the analysis. The regression results narrowed those signals further, with reduced working hours remaining statistically significant.

The paper interprets these results within the extraordinary conditions of the pandemic, when digital work, health concerns, income pressures, organizational recovery, and changing work arrangements were all affecting employees and firms simultaneously.

Study scope

The findings came from a small, non-random organizational sample gathered during an unusual economic period.

The manufacturing and retail analysis used 18 organizational respondents selected through purposive and convenience sampling. The manuscript explicitly notes that the findings should not be generalized to all organizations in those industries.

The retention measure was derived from organizational estimates of employee numbers in 2020 and 2021 rather than from individual employee-level longitudinal data.

The paper also reports that the retention-rate distribution was not perfectly normal, which introduces additional caution when interpreting the parametric regression models.

Future research

The paper recommends testing HR practices with broader samples and more direct measures of effectiveness.

The manuscript recommends further research on recruitment effectiveness, training and development, performance appraisal, and the use of online tools in HRM during pandemic conditions.

It also suggests examining skills enhancement training more closely because the practice showed a positive bivariate relationship with retention in the manufacturing and retail subset, even though that relationship was not retained in the multiple-regression model.
