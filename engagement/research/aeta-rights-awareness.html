Research Manuscript · Psychology 101 · 2019

Ang Tulog na Karapatan ng mga Taong Binubura sa Mapa: A Study on the Awareness of the Aeta Community on their Indigenous People's Rights

My first research paper in Psychology 101 examined how members of an Aeta community understood, experienced, and talked about their rights, including awareness of the Indigenous Peoples’ Rights Act.

The question

What did members of the community know about their rights, and how did that knowledge meet lived experience?

The study investigated the level of awareness of Aeta respondents regarding their rights, particularly those associated with the Indigenous Peoples’ Rights Act.

It asked what respondents knew, how they believed they were able to exercise those rights, and what experiences shaped their sense of whether those rights were respected in practice.

Rights awareness was therefore treated not only as knowledge of a law, but as something connected to land, livelihood, education, voting, healthcare, security, and everyday encounters with institutions.

Method

The project brought introductory psychology research into a living community context.

The manuscript describes a qualitative descriptive approach. Fieldwork was conducted in Macapagal Village, where the researchers first sought permission from community leaders before interviewing participants.

Oral surveys and semi-structured interviews were used, with pakikipagkwentuhan and pagtatanong identified as culturally responsive ways of eliciting participants' experiences and understanding.

The researchers also used field notes and later compared interview material with the literature. The paper's analysis combined narrative interpretation with descriptive counts and charts.

What the study reported

Knowing that rights exist was not the same as knowing how to use them.

The findings below summarize the manuscript's own reporting of the 16-person study and should be read within that local context.

01

Awareness of IPRA was limited

Six respondents were reported to have heard of IPRA, while only two were described as knowing how to use it in relation to concrete needs or disputes.

02

Rights were understood through everyday life

Respondents commonly described rights through access to livelihood, education, voting, healthcare, land, shelter, freedom, and the ability to live peacefully.

03

Land and resettlement were recurring concerns

Fifteen of the 16 respondents were reported to have been asked to consider resettlement, while several interviews connected rights awareness to ancestral land, documentation, and security of tenure.

04

Awareness did not guarantee power

Several respondents described knowing that they had rights while still feeling unable to exercise them consistently or defend them against more powerful institutions and actors.

Reading the study responsibly

The paper is an early field study with useful evidence and visible methodological limits.

The study involved only 16 respondents from one community, and most participants were women engaged in similar livelihood activities. Its findings should not be generalized to all Aeta communities.

The manuscript is also internally inconsistent about sampling: the methodology describes snowball sampling, while the results section says respondents were chosen through purposive sampling.

Researchers sometimes explained IPRA during the interviews before asking later questions about what participants could do with that knowledge. That makes the later answers useful as reflections after an intervention, but not clean measures of prior awareness.

The paper itself recommends broader occupational representation, more balanced participation by men and women, interviews with relevant authorities, greater language capacity among interviewers, and a larger sample in future research.

What changed for me

Psychology became less abstract when the research question had to survive contact with people's lives.

This was my first research paper in Psychology 101. Compared with classroom discussion, fieldwork made research feel less like a sequence of academic steps and more like a responsibility to listen carefully, ask better questions, and notice the distance between a formal right and a lived reality.

It also introduced me to research choices that would matter again in later work: informed consent, community entry, language, culturally grounded interviewing, translation, and the limits of what a small qualitative study can claim.

Looking back, the paper is valuable to me not because every part of the design was mature, but because it marks an early point where psychology, institutions, culture, and lived experience began to meet in the same research problem.
