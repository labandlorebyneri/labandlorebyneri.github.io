Research Manuscript · Qualitative Research · June 2021

Pakikipagkapwa and other Filipino values and Individual Filipino’s Perception of Own Responsibility in the Community Pantry Phenomenon Amidst the COVID-19 Crisis

A qualitative study of how organizers and donors understood community pantries through pakikipagkapwa, mutual responsibility, volunteerism, and collective action during the COVID-19 crisis.

The questions

What motivated participation, and how did pakikipagkapwa shape a sense of responsibility?

The study asked what motivated organizers and donors to participate in community pantries, what role pakikipagkapwa played in their perception of personal responsibility, and how they described their experiences within the phenomenon.

Rather than treating community pantries only as emergency food distribution, the paper examined them as a socio-cultural phenomenon shaped by relationships, shared identity, volunteerism, and ideas about responsibility to others.

Method

The study used a virtual focus group to examine meaning as something constructed with others.

The researchers used a qualitative paradigm and a social constructionist approach, augmented by Virgilio Enriquez’s Kapwa Theory. Participants were Filipino community pantry organizers and donors aged 18–59.

Recruitment used purposive and snowball sampling through community-pantry social-media spaces. Ten sign-ups were screened; five participants remained for the focus group after exclusions based on eligibility, willingness, and availability.

The FGD was conducted via Zoom. I served as moderator, Maria Theresa B. Largo handled technical checks and participant support, and Adriel Jireh M. Lumague recorded field notes and observations.

Data analysis used Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis. Researchers first familiarized themselves with the transcript and field notes, generated initial codes individually, and then met to retain, combine, or revise themes.

Research scope

The study examined how Filipino values shaped participation, responsibility, and collective action in community pantries.

The manuscript explores the experiences and motivations of community pantry organizers and donors through the lens of Sikolohiyang Pilipino, with particular attention to pakikipagkapwa and related Filipino values.

Reading the study responsibly

The themes are rich, but the study was deliberately small and context-bound.

This was a small qualitative study conducted during pandemic restrictions, so its claims are context-bound and should not be treated as population-level conclusions.

The researchers used reflexivity and participant-validation practices to make assumptions and interpretations visible during the research process.

Additional methodological and interpretive detail is intentionally withheld from this public page while the manuscript is under publication consideration.

My contribution

This paper taught me that facilitation is part of research, not separate from it.

I served as FGD moderator and co-author. That meant carrying the interview guide while also managing pacing, consent, language preference, participation, respect among participants, and the possibility of conflict.

The research design made that role especially important because pakikipagpalagayang-loob and a level relationship of mutual trust were treated as part of the data-collection process rather than as mere etiquette.

The experience strengthened an idea that has continued into later work: listening is not passive. Good qualitative research depends on creating enough structure for people to speak freely, and enough restraint for the researcher not to become the center of what is being said.
