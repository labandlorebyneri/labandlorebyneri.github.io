The Status of Mental Health in the Filipino LGBTQ+ Research Landscape: A Scoping Review

A scoping review that mapped how Philippine LGBTQ+ research from 2000–2019 reflected mental health and what recurring psychological and social challenges appeared across the literature.

The questions

How much of Philippine LGBTQ+ research reflected mental health, and what patterns appeared across the literature?

The review asked two questions: to what extent LGBTQ+ research in the Philippines reflected mental health, and what recurring patterns or mental-health-related dilemmas appeared in the experiences represented across those studies.

The project was designed as a mapping exercise rather than a clinical prevalence study. Its purpose was to understand what the existing research landscape was paying attention to, what methods it used, and where important gaps remained.

Method

The review moved from a broad search to a smaller body of eligible Philippine studies.

The researchers searched Google Scholar, ProQuest, and Academia.edu, then supplemented the search with relevant articles from a Philippine Journal of Psychology special issue and forward/backward searching.

Eligible sources were Philippine LGBTQ+ studies written in Filipino or English, published or unpublished between 2000 and 2019, and using qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-method designs.

The manuscript reports 909 records identified initially, 95 full-text articles assessed for eligibility, and 26 studies included in the final review.

Data were charted across study objectives, methods, sampling, participants, data-collection procedures, and significant findings. The review used a PRISMA-informed screening process and an interpretive synthesis of recurring patterns across included studies.

What the review mapped

The literature was methodologically uneven, but several patterns recurred.

The counts below are reported from the manuscript's results sections. Where the paper contains internal inconsistencies across sections, this page avoids forcing a single reconciled percentage.

01

Qualitative studies were common

The final review included 26 studies, with 15 described as qualitative and 11 as quantitative in the manuscript's results section.

02

Gay participants were most represented

The review found that studies involving gay participants appeared more frequently than those centered on bisexual, transgender, or trans women participants.

03

Mental health appeared in most included studies

Nineteen of the 26 studies were coded as reflecting mental-health issues, while eight included suicide-related issues.

04

Rejection repeatedly appeared across studies

Rejection from family, peers, or other people was one of the most common recurring patterns identified across the included literature.

Research gaps

Mapping the literature also showed who and what the literature was missing.

The manuscript notes that the evidence base was weighted toward studies of lesbian and gay participants, with less representation of other LGBTQ+ groups.

It also identifies access constraints: only a limited number of databases were used, some relevant sources were inaccessible, and the availability of local LGBTQ+ research was still comparatively sparse.

These limitations matter because a scoping review can only map the evidence it is able to retrieve. Gaps in access and representation can therefore become gaps in the picture the review is able to produce.

Reading the study responsibly

The review is useful as a map of a 2019 evidence landscape, not as a current estimate of LGBTQ+ mental health.

The search ended with literature available through 2019, so the review should be read as a historical snapshot of what the authors could find at that time rather than as a description of the current research landscape.

The manuscript also contains a few internal inconsistencies in counts and percentages across the abstract, figures, synthesis, and conclusion. This public summary therefore emphasizes the more stable counts and broad patterns rather than presenting every percentage as definitive.

The review did not conduct a critical appraisal or formal risk-of-bias assessment of included studies. Its primary purpose was descriptive mapping.

What changed for me

This project shifted research from studying one phenomenon to studying the shape of an entire evidence base.

Unlike field research, a scoping review required us to treat published studies themselves as data: to search systematically, decide what belonged, chart comparable features, and look for patterns across different methods and populations.

It made visible a different kind of research question. Sometimes the problem is not only what people are experiencing, but what the existing literature has repeatedly studied, what it has neglected, and how those choices shape what a field thinks it knows.
