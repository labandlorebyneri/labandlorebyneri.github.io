Research Manuscript · Psych 115 · 2020

Uy May Chika Ako!: Likelihood of Voting as Influenced by the Electoral Candidate's Background and the Type of Relationship with the Communication Source

A Psych 115 experimental study examining whether the type of controversy attached to a hypothetical electoral candidate and the participant's relationship with a communication source influenced university students' reported likelihood of voting.

Academic context
Psych 115 · Experimental Research in Psychology · University of the Philippines
Manuscript date
22 December 2020
Authors
Faith Ayuyao, Kobe Bagaipo, Joshua Neri Cristobal, and Jacqueline Marie Dimalanta
My role
Co-author
Research design
3 × 2 between-subjects factorial experiment
Participants
150 university students, ages 18-22, from UP Diliman and other higher education institutions in the Philippines
Independent variables
Relationship with communication source: stranger, close friend, or professor; candidate controversy: corruption or infidelity
Outcome measure
Self-reported likelihood of voting using a 5-point Likert scale
Analysis
Two-way independent-measures ANOVA, with bootstrapping used after normality and homogeneity assumptions were violated

The question

Do candidate controversies and social relationships change reported willingness to vote?

The experiment tested two possible influences on voting likelihood. The first was the type of relationship participants were asked to imagine having with a person who supported a candidate. The second was the type of negative background information attached to the hypothetical candidate.

Participants encountered one of three communication sources, a stranger, a close friend, or a professor, and one of two candidate controversies, corruption or infidelity. The study also tested whether the two factors interacted.

Experimental design

Six conditions crossed social relationship with candidate controversy.

The study used a 3 × 2 between-subjects factorial design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of six experimental conditions, with 25 participants in each condition.

The relationship manipulation described the communication source as either a stranger, a close friend, or a respected professor. The candidate-background manipulation presented a hypothetical news headline involving either corruption or infidelity.

Participants then read a vignette in which the communication source said they would still vote for the candidate despite the controversy. Afterwards, participants rated how likely they themselves would be to vote for that candidate.

Participants & procedure

The experiment was conducted remotely during December 2020.

Data collection took place from December 4 to December 12, 2020. Recruitment included Psych 101 students at UP Diliman, other UP Diliman undergraduates, and college students from other universities.

The final sample contained 150 participants between 18 and 22 years old. The manuscript reports that 124 participants were UP students and 26 were non-UP students.

Google Forms hosted the experimental materials, consent form, manipulation checks, vignette, outcome measure, filler questions, and debriefing materials.

What the study found

The type of candidate controversy mattered. Relationship type did not reach statistical significance.

The findings below summarize the manuscript's reported results for this 150-participant university-student sample.

01

Issue type had a significant main effect

Participants reported a higher likelihood of voting in the infidelity condition than in the corruption condition. The manuscript reports means of 1.97 for infidelity and 1.47 for corruption.

02

Relationship type was not significant

Mean voting-likelihood scores increased from stranger to close friend to professor, but the overall effect of relationship with the communication source was not statistically significant.

03

No interaction effect was established

The effect of candidate controversy did not significantly change depending on whether the communication source was a stranger, close friend, or professor.

04

Overall voting likelihood remained low

Across the experimental conditions, the reported means remained toward the lower end of the 5-point scale, even when the candidate was described positively in other respects.

Statistical analysis

The manuscript used bootstrapping after assumption tests raised concerns.

Before the two-way ANOVA, the researchers tested normality and homogeneity of variance. Both tests indicated violations of the relevant assumptions.

The manuscript therefore reports using 1,000 bootstrap samples as a robust approach alongside the ANOVA. The reported main effect of controversy type was significant, while the main effect of relationship type and the interaction between the two variables were not.

Interpretation

The experiment distinguished reactions to different kinds of candidate misconduct more clearly than reactions to different social sources.

Within this experimental setup, participants differentiated between the two candidate controversies. Corruption produced lower reported voting likelihood than infidelity.

By contrast, being told that a stranger, close friend, or professor supported the candidate did not produce a statistically significant difference in voting likelihood. The manuscript discusses this as evidence that participants' judgments may have been more resistant to interpersonal influence than initially hypothesized.

These interpretations belong to the study's original 2020 discussion and should be read within the limits of its design and sample.

Study scope

The experiment tested a specific hypothetical decision among university students.

The sample was limited to young adults enrolled in higher education, with UP students comprising most of the participants. The experiment was also conducted remotely during the shift to online learning, which reduced control over participants' physical environments.

The dependent measure captured stated likelihood of voting in a hypothetical scenario rather than an observed vote in an actual election. The study also compared only two controversy types and three communication-source relationships.

The manuscript recommends future work with more diverse samples, physical experimental settings where possible, broader relationship categories, additional candidate-background conditions, and wider measures of voting behavior.
