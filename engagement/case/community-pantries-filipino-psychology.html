Community Pantries Through Filipino Psychology

A qualitative study of community pantries during the COVID-19 pandemic, and an early lesson in how everyday behavior can become evidence for understanding values, collective responsibility, and social response.

Year: 2021

← Back to Research & Public Conversation

Why this case matters

Ordinary behavior can reveal extraordinary social questions.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, community pantries became visible across the Philippines as ordinary citizens organized ways of sharing food and other necessities.

Our research asked what these acts could tell us about Filipino values, collective responsibility, and how people respond when formal systems are under strain.

For me, the importance of the work was not only the subject itself. It was learning to treat something happening in everyday life as a legitimate object of scientific inquiry rather than as a passing social moment.

The research

What can collective behavior tell us about values?

The study examined the community pantry phenomenon as an expression of Filipino values and as a form of ordinary citizens' call for social change during the pandemic.

The subject mattered because community pantries sat at the intersection of material need, mutual aid, civic participation, and questions about how people respond when institutions and communities are under pressure.

Looking at the phenomenon through Sikolohiyang Pilipino also meant taking Filipino concepts and experiences seriously as part of the analytical frame rather than treating them as secondary to imported categories.

From observation to evidence

Research begins when we stop assuming we already understand what we are seeing.

Community pantries were highly visible at the time. Their visibility could make them feel immediately understandable: people needed help, other people shared what they had, and communities organized around that need.

Research required more discipline than that first explanation. It meant asking what values were being expressed, how people understood their own participation, and what larger social meanings could be interpreted from those actions without reducing them to a single story.

That distinction stayed with me. Familiarity is not the same as understanding. Something can happen in front of us every day and still deserve closer inquiry.

Public conversation

Presenting the work meant allowing our interpretation to be questioned.

I co-authored the study and served as its paper presenter at the conference, communicating the research, its findings, and its implications to participants working within Filipino psychology.

That shifted the work from a research exercise into a wider scholarly conversation. Once presented publicly, our interpretation no longer belonged only to the people who conducted the study.

It could be compared with other work, questioned by people with different expertise, and situated within a broader conversation about Filipino behavior, values, and social life.

That is one reason I place this case under Renaissance. Research does not end when an answer has been written. Its value grows when the answer is exposed to other people capable of making it more precise.

Renaissance

Everyday life can become evidence when we learn how to ask better questions of it.

The community pantry was not only a response to material need. It opened questions about kapwa, collective responsibility, civic participation, and what people do when existing systems are under strain.

Research gave us a way to examine those questions systematically. Public conversation gave other people a chance to challenge how we understood them.

What I carry forward

Curiosity can turn something familiar into something worth understanding again.

This experience showed me that scientific inquiry does not always begin with something distant or unfamiliar. Sometimes the most useful questions are hiding inside behaviors we have already normalized.

Community pantries were visible, immediate, and deeply connected to the conditions of the pandemic. Research created distance from that immediacy long enough to ask what the phenomenon might reveal about Filipino values and collective response.

Presenting the work then returned those questions to a community of scholars and practitioners, where our interpretation could be tested again.

That cycle of observation, inquiry, interpretation, and exchange is still how I think about research: not as a way to make an idea untouchable, but as a way to make it more accountable to evidence and conversation.
