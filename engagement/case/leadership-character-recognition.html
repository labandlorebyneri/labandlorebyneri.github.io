Leadership & Character Recognition

A case about being doubted, challenging narrow standards of who gets to lead, and learning that recognition matters most when it confirms what resistance could not erase.

Records: 2016 · 2018 · 2023

← Back to Recognition Through Service

Why this case matters

The question was never whether I could lead. It was who was allowed to be seen as a leader.

I have always been willing to question standards that are treated as natural simply because they are familiar. In junior high school, that became personal. I was doubted as someone who could lead the student council because I was seen as "different" within the expectations of a Catholic-school environment.

What stayed with me was not the doubt itself, but what it revealed. Sometimes people are judged against an image of leadership before they are judged by what they can actually do.

I did not think being different made me less capable. If anything, it made me more certain that the standard deserved to be challenged.

2016

I learned early that leadership can be judged before it is tested.

In junior high school, I entered student leadership already aware that some people did not think I fit the expected mold. I was different by the standards around me, and that difference became part of the conversation about whether I should be trusted to lead.

But I had a simpler standard. Could I organize? Could I communicate? Could I take responsibility? Could I show up for people and do the work?

Those were the questions that mattered to me. Leadership, I began to understand, is not about matching an approved image. It is about what you are willing to carry once people depend on you.

History repeats

Senior high school brought the same question back with higher stakes.

Years later, another student-election environment brought the same tension back. I experienced what felt like coordinated opposition from some teachers, who openly supported another candidate. She happened to be a friend I had known since elementary school.

The conflict was never really about her. She was not the problem. What stayed with me was the larger experience of seeing authority figures participate in deciding who looked more acceptable, more appropriate, or more deserving of leadership.

By then, I understood that the real confrontation was not between two students. It was with the assumptions around us about who was allowed to represent a community.

I could not control who doubted me, who campaigned against me, or which standards they used. I could control whether I adopted those standards as my own.

I did not.

The final test

The awards came last.

The arc came full circle when I was nominated for the AY Foundation National Discipline Award. By then, I had already spent years learning how to lead while being questioned, measured, and sometimes resisted.

I was surprised when I was also awarded the Bishop's Award. The recognition mattered, but not because it suddenly proved that I had been capable all along. I had never doubted that.

What made the moment powerful was that the same institution in which I had repeatedly felt pressure to fit a narrow standard was now publicly affirming the contribution I had made without becoming someone else first.

Reflection

Recognition did not create the confidence. It arrived after it.

Looking back, I sometimes remember those years through the image of David facing several Goliaths, with spectators watching to see whether the smaller figure would eventually accept the scale of the odds.

I never did.

There was sweetness in the victory, but the deeper lesson was not that I had finally been proven right. It was that self-belief had to come before permission, before applause, and before an award.

What I carry forward

The award was not permission.

The most important part of this story is not that recognition eventually came. It is that I had already learned not to let other people's standards become the boundary of my own possibility.

Recognition can still matter. It can correct a record. It can affirm contribution. It can show that a community's definition of who belongs is capable of changing.

But recognition should not become the source of confidence. By the time the awards arrived, the deeper victory had already happened. I had continued to lead without waiting to be made acceptable first.

That is the part I carry forward: challenge the standard when the standard is too small for the people it is supposed to serve.
