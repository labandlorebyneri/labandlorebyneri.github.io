College Readiness in the New Normal

My first invited talk became a reflection on what college had taught me beyond academics: that making it through is not only an individual act of endurance. We survive, adjust, and grow with other people.

Why this case matters

College taught me that survival is not only personal. It is something we learn to do together.

On January 28, 2021, I returned to St. John’s Academy Inc., my alma mater, as one of the resource speakers for Career Guidance Week. I was still a BA Psychology student at UP Diliman.

It was my first invited talk.

I spoke about how college had changed me both as a student and as a person. Part of that change was learning to be kinder to myself while still taking my academic goals seriously. But the larger lesson was realizing that college is not something we survive alone.

The talk

College readiness is not only about learning how to survive. It is about learning how to survive together.

I shared practical advice on navigating college and adapting to a new environment, but the part that mattered most to me was the idea that readiness is relational.

College had challenged the version of achievement I had carried for years. I still wanted to do well. I still cared about academic excellence. But I was beginning to understand that endurance becomes more sustainable when it is shared through friendships, classmates, mentors, family, and the small communities we build around ourselves.

Being kinder to myself was part of that lesson, but so was learning to receive help, offer it, ask questions, compare notes, celebrate other people's progress, and recognize when someone else needed support. Surviving college was never only about becoming individually stronger. It was also about learning how to become part of a network of people trying to make it through together.

Reflection

The first time I was asked to speak, I realized that what helped me survive college was never mine alone.

There was something circular about being invited back to the school where I had once been the student listening to adults describe the future.

When I entered college, I quickly became aware that people were arriving from very different starting points. Some classmates from science high schools had deeper preparation in mathematics and science. Others who came from larger, highly established schools seemed more confident in what they could already do.

I could not change the preparation I arrived with. What I did have was self-awareness. I knew where I was struggling, which subjects demanded more time from me, and when I needed to stop pretending that effort alone meant working by myself.

So I learned to stay longer with what I did not understand and to ask classmates I trusted for help. Their strengths did not diminish mine. They gave me another way to learn. In return, I began to understand that resilience was not simply the ability to endure difficulty on my own. It was also the ability to recognize a limitation, seek support, and keep moving without turning comparison into shame.

That became the story I wanted to return to younger students: college can feel intensely personal, but very little of it is survived alone. Different people will begin with different advantages. Self-awareness helps us recognize our own starting point, and community gives us more ways to grow from it.

Education and development, for me, are not simply about transferring information. They are also about making knowledge, encouragement, and support easier to pass from one person to another. Sometimes the most useful thing we can offer is not a formula for success, but evidence that asking for help, helping others, and moving together are also forms of strength.

Character → Reflections

Growth became visible when ambition stopped being a solitary exercise.

The achievement itself was not the lesson. The deeper lesson was self-awareness: knowing what I could do, recognizing what I still needed to learn, and becoming secure enough to ask other people to help me close the distance.

My first talk gave me a chance to return that lesson to students standing at the beginning of their own transition. College may test individual endurance, but we do not have to make endurance an individual burden. Sometimes character is knowing when persistence means trying harder, and when it means trusting someone enough to learn with them.
